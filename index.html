One bad year in one stock is two and a half withdrawal years

A retirement can be fully indexed and still carry concentrated risk. The usual shape: half the portfolio in a total-market fund, half in the stock of the company that paid the salary, RSUs that never got sold because the chart kept going up. Two buckets, $500,000 each, and then the year the company stock drops 40 percent while the index drops 18.

The concentrated plan lands at $710,000. The all-index plan lands at $820,000. Same market year, same starting balance, $110,000 apart. At $40,000 a year, the gap is two and three quarter years of withdrawals.

The hole is deeper than the loss. The stock bucket has to gain 66.7 percent to see $500,000 again. The index bucket needs 22 percent. The required regain runs three times higher on the concentrated half, and withdrawals do not pause while the regain happens. Money pulled out of a down balance never compounds back.

Run the ledger forward fifteen years at a 5 percent return with $40,000 coming out each year. The concentrated plan finishes near $613,000. The all-index plan finishes near $842,000. A $229,000 gap, priced on the day the allocation was chosen.

Rank the decision the way every other retirement decision gets ranked: shortfall in withdrawal-years for the bad year, regain required to heal, balance left at the end of the horizon. The employer stock clears those numbers or it gets sold down.
